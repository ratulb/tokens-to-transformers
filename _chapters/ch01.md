**This chapter covers**

- Why a language model never sees text, only integers
- Byte pair encoding (BPE), worked by hand and then in Mojo
- Building a small BPE tokenizer, and breaking it on purpose
- The production tokenizer, `mbpe`, and the ideas that make it different
- Checking our tokenizer against the published GPT-2 token IDs

A GPT-2 model cannot read. It has no idea what a letter is.

It reads integers. Everything this book builds, attention, the model, the training loop, starts from a list of them. This chapter produces that list.

Let's begin at the end, with the finished tokenizer.

## 1.1 Text in, IDs out

The tokenizer we end up with is `mbpe`. Here it is loading the GPT-2 vocabulary and round-tripping a string:

```mojo
from bpe.tokenizer import Tokenizers


def main() raises:
    var gpt2 = Tokenizers.get[Tokenizers.gpt2]()
    var ids = gpt2.encode("hello world")
    print(gpt2.decode(ids))
```

*Source: the Mojo API example in the `mbpe` README.*

And the same tokenizer from Python, which shows the IDs:

```python
import mbpe

tokenizer = mbpe.get_encoding("gpt2")
tokens = tokenizer.encode("hello world")

print(tokens)                   # [31373, 995]
print(tokenizer.decode(tokens)) # "hello world"
```

*Source: the first example in the `mbpe` README.*

Eleven characters in. Two integers out: `31373` and `995`. Those are the same two IDs OpenAI's GPT-2 produces for the same string.

Where the tokenizer sits matters, so here is the whole path:

```text
"hello world" -> [tokenizer] -> [31373, 995] -> model -> [31373, 995] -> [tokenizer] -> "hello world"
```

The tokenizer stands on both ends of the model. Everything between the arrows works on IDs. Embeddings are looked up by ID. Attention mixes IDs. The output layer scores IDs. The model has no idea how the text was split.

That decision is made earlier, once, when the tokenizer is trained on a corpus and the mapping is frozen. ID `31373` means `hello` for GPT-2 because the weights were trained against that mapping. Change the mapping and the weights are meaningless.

> **Reading the Mojo.** The listings use a handful of Mojo constructs throughout. `var` declares a mutable local. `def … raises` means the function can throw an error. A trailing `^` moves ownership out of the function instead of copying. `ref` borrows a value without copying it. `comptime` marks a compile-time constant, and `Struct[T]` in `BPETokenizer[T]` or `Tokenizers.get[T]` passes the pre-tokenizer as a compile-time parameter, so each choice compiles to its own specialized code. `List` and `Dict` are the growable array and hash table.

Look at what the two IDs actually are: `hello` and ` world`. The space belongs to the second token. `world` without it is a different token, `6894`. Put two spaces between the words and you get three IDs, `[31373, 220, 995]`: the extra space becomes a token of its own. The tokenizer encodes exact bytes, not words.

Whether something is one token is a property of the vocabulary, not of the thing. Under GPT-2's vocabulary the Hindi word `नमस्ते` becomes twelve IDs, each a fragment of its UTF-8 bytes, and `😀` becomes two. The vocabulary never learned them whole. A tokenizer that did would spend fewer IDs on the same text. That difference is a tax on some languages, and Exercise 1.5 lets you measure it.

The rest of the chapter explains where those numbers come from. We do it in the order a reader would want: first the idea, then a small implementation you can read in one sitting, then the real thing.

## 1.2 Why not words, and why not characters

Two obvious designs come to mind. Both fail, in opposite directions.

**One ID per word.** The vocabulary is a dictionary of every word in the training text. This is easy to build and it breaks the first time the model sees a word it has not seen. A tokenizer built on a short story has no ID for "Hello" if the story never says it. Real text is full of names, typos, code identifiers and other languages. A word-level vocabulary either grows without bound or throws information away.

**One ID per character.** Now nothing is ever unknown. But sequences get long: the model must piece words together from letters before it can do anything useful, and attention compares every position with every other, so wasted length is expensive.

Subword tokenization sits between the two. Common words become a single token. Rare words are spelled out in pieces. Nothing is ever unknown.

| Representation | Vocabulary | Sequence length | Unseen words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Words | Large | Short | Poor |
| Characters or bytes | Small | Long | Excellent |
| Subwords, with a byte fallback | Moderate | Moderate | Excellent |

Two costs explain why neither extreme survives.

**Length is expensive.** Attention compares every position with every other. Fifty tokens means 2,500 pairs. A hundred means 10,000. Spelling everything in characters pays that bill for nothing, and makes the model relearn that `t-h-e` is a word.

**Vocabulary is expensive too.** Every entry owns a row in the embedding table. GPT-2's smallest model uses 768 dimensions, so its 50,257 rows hold about 39 million numbers before a single attention layer exists. A bigger vocabulary buys shorter sequences and fatter tables. The tokenizer spends parameters before the transformer sees anything.

BPE is the most widely used way to find those pieces, and the one GPT-2 uses.

## 1.3 BPE by hand

BPE learns its pieces from data. The training procedure fits in five lines:

1. Split the text into words and count how often each word occurs.
2. Start the vocabulary with every distinct character.
3. Count every adjacent pair of tokens, weighted by word frequency.
4. Merge the most frequent pair into one new token and remember the rule.
5. Repeat from step 3 until the vocabulary is the size you asked for.

Here is the example from the Hugging Face course. Five words, with counts:

| word | count |
|---|---|
| hug | 10 |
| pug | 5 |
| pun | 12 |
| bun | 4 |
| hugs | 5 |

The starting vocabulary is the characters `b g h n p s u`. Count the adjacent pairs:

| pair | count | where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| u g | 20 | hug (10) + pug (5) + hugs (5) |
| p u | 17 | pug (5) + pun (12) |
| u n | 16 | pun (12) + bun (4) |
| h u | 15 | hug (10) + hugs (5) |

`u g` wins. It becomes a new token, `ug`, and every word is rewritten:

```text
h ug    p ug    p u n    b u n    h ug s
```

Count again. Now `u n` leads with 16, so `un` is the second token. After that, `h ug` has 15, so `hug` is the third:

```text
hug    p ug    p un    b un    hug s
```

Three merges, three new tokens, three rules, **in this order**:

```text
(u, g) -> ug
(u, n) -> un
(h, ug) -> hug
```

The order matters. The third rule builds on the first: `hug` cannot exist until `ug` does.

To encode new text, split it into characters and replay the rules in the order they were learned. Take `bug`:

```text
b u g   ->  b ug      after rule 1
            b ug      rules 2 and 3 find nothing
```

Two tokens: `b` and `ug`. Each token has an ID, its position in the vocabulary, and the list of IDs is what the model receives.

That is the whole algorithm. The rest of the chapter is making it work on real text.

## 1.4 A tokenizer you can read

`simple_bpe` is 431 lines of Mojo in one file. It trains, encodes, decodes, saves and loads. We look at the parts that map to the napkin.

### Cutting text into words

BPE only merges inside a word, so the text must be cut into words first. `simple_bpe` uses two rules: every space becomes a marker, `Ġ`, glued to the front of the next word, and every period becomes its own word. The marker is the one GPT-2 uses. It lets the decoder restore each space exactly.

*`simple_bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 98–112 (the body of `PreTokenizer.split`)*

```mojo
        var splits = (
            StringSlice(text)
            # " " -> " Ġ": keep a space as the cut point and add the marker after it.
            .replace(" ", " " + spacer)
            # "." -> " .": add a cut point before every period.
            .replace(".", " .")
            # Cut at every space. The separators vanish; the marker stays.
            .split(" ")
        )
        # The pieces are string views into the text above. Copy each one into
        # its own String so the result owns its data.
        var result = List[String](capacity=len(splits))
        for split in splits:
            result.append(String(from_utf8=split.as_bytes()))
        return result^
```

`"hello world."` becomes `["hello", "Ġworld", "."]`. Nothing else is handled. Commas, digits, contractions and newlines stay attached to whatever is next to them. We will come back to that.

### The training loop

Steps 3, 4 and 5 of the recipe are this loop. Each pass adds exactly one token to the vocabulary.

*`simple_bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 188–215*

```mojo
        # Step 5. The merge loop. Each pass adds exactly one vocabulary entry.
        self.merges = Dict[Tuple[String, String], String]()
        while len(self.vocab) < vocab_size:
            # Count every adjacent pair across all words, weighted by how often
            # each word occurs.
            var pair_freqs = _compute_pair_freqs(splits, word_freqs)
            # No pairs means every word is already a single token.
            if len(pair_freqs) == 0:
                break
            # Find the most frequent pair. The comparison is strict (<), so on a
            # tie the pair seen first in iteration order stays the winner.
            # max_freq == -1 only matters on the first pair: it accepts it.
            var best_pair: Tuple[String, String] = ("", "")
            var max_freq = -1
            for pair_freq in pair_freqs.items():
                var pair = pair_freq.key
                var freq = pair_freq.value
                if max_freq == -1 or max_freq < freq:
                    best_pair = pair
                    max_freq = freq
            # Rewrite every word so the winning pair becomes one token.
            _merge_pair(best_pair[0], best_pair[1], splits, word_freqs)
            # Record the new token. Appending to vocab gives it the next free
            # ID, and inserting into merges keeps the rules in learned order.
            var joined = best_pair[0].copy() + best_pair[1].copy()
            self.merges[best_pair] = joined
            self.vocab.append(joined)
            self.stoi[joined] = len(self.vocab) - 1
```

Read it against the napkin. `_compute_pair_freqs` is the pair table:

*`simple_bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 380–392 (the body of `_compute_pair_freqs`)*

```mojo
    var pair_freqs = Dict[Tuple[String, String], Int]()
    for word_freq in word_freqs.items():
        var word = word_freq.key
        var freq = word_freq.value
        ref split = splits[word]
        # A word reduced to one token has no pairs left.
        if len(split) == 1:
            continue
        # Pair each token with the one to its right.
        for i in range(len(split) - 1):
            var pair = (split[i], split[i + 1])
            pair_freqs[pair] = pair_freqs.get(pair, 0) + freq
    return pair_freqs^
```

`_merge_pair` is the rewrite from `u g` to `ug`:

*`simple_bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 412–431 (the body of `_merge_pair`)*

```mojo
    for word in word_freqs:
        ref split = splits[word]
        if len(split) == 1:
            continue
        var i = 0
        while i < len(split) - 1:
            if split[i] == a and split[i + 1] == b:
                # Rebuild the list: everything before i, the joined token,
                # everything after i + 1. The merged token is at i, so i stays
                # put. The joined token never equals a, so it cannot match
                # again at the same position, and the next pass moves on.
                split = (
                    [e for e in split[:i]]
                    + [a + b]
                    + [e for e in split[i + 2 :]]
                )
            else:
                i += 1
        # Store the updated list back under the word.
        splits[word] = split.copy()
```

Two details worth noticing. The pair counts are weighted by word frequency, so a word that appears ten times counts ten times. And when two pairs tie, the one seen first wins. That is a simple rule, and it makes training deterministic: the same corpus gives the same merges.

### Encoding is replaying

Encoding walks the merge rules in the order they were learned, and applies each one to every word.

*`simple_bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 232–267 (the body of `_tokenize`)*

```mojo
        if text.byte_length() == 0:
            return List[String]()
        # Same word split as in training. The words must be cut the same way,
        # or the learned rules would not line up with the pieces.
        var words = PreTokenizer.split(text)
        # One list of single-character tokens per word.
        var splits = [
            [chr(Int(cp)) for cp in word.codepoints()]
            for word in words
        ]
        # merges.items() yields rules in the order they were inserted, which
        # is the order they were learned.
        for pair_merge in self.merges.items():
            ref pair = pair_merge.key
            ref merge = pair_merge.value
            for idx, split in enumerate(splits):
                var i = 0
                var split_copied = split.copy()
                # Walk the word left to right looking for the rule's pair.
                while i < len(split_copied) - 1:
                    if split_copied[i] == pair[0] and split_copied[i + 1] == pair[1]:
                        # Replace the two tokens with the joined token: keep
                        # everything before i, insert the joined token, keep
                        # everything after i + 1. i is not advanced; the
                        # joined token sits at i and is compared with its new
                        # right neighbour on the next pass.
                        split_copied = (
                            [e for e in split_copied[:i]]
                            + [merge]
                            + [e for e in split_copied[i + 2 :]]
                        )
                    else:
                        i += 1
                splits[idx] = split_copied^
        # Join the per-word lists into one flat list of tokens.
        return [item for sublist in splits for item in sublist]
```

This is the napkin procedure, written out. It is also, as we will see, the slow way to do it.

## 1.5 Limits of the small tokenizer

A tokenizer written to be read has shortcuts. Three of them matter, and each one shows up on real text.

### Unseen characters

`simple_bpe` starts its vocabulary from the characters in the training text. Anything else has no entry. Its answer is a placeholder token, `<UNK>`, at ID 0.

The test says it directly. Train on `"abc"`, then encode `"xyz"`:

*`simple_bpe/main.mojo`, lines 5–15*

```mojo
def test_unk_fallback() raises:
    var corpus = List[String]()
    corpus.append(String("abc"))
    var tok = BPETokenizer()
    tok.train(corpus, 12)
    var ids = tok.encode(String("xyz"))
    assert_equal(len(ids), 3)
    assert_equal(ids[0], 0)
    assert_equal(ids[1], 0)
    assert_equal(ids[2], 0)
    assert_equal(tok.decode(ids), "<UNK><UNK><UNK>")
```

Three characters in, three `<UNK>` out. The text is gone, and no decoder can bring it back. A model trained on this tokenizer cannot tell "xyz" from any other three unknown characters. The Hugging Face course version raises an error here instead.

### A pre-tokenizer with two rules

Spaces and periods. That is all the pre-tokenizer in section 1.4 handles. `"don't"`, `"hello,"` and the newline in `"line one\nline two"` all stay glued to the words beside them, in chunks that no model should be asked to learn from. GPT-2's tokenizer separates letters from digits from punctuation from whitespace, and its merges were learned under that splitting. Without the same splitting, the same merges do not apply.

### Encoding by replaying every rule

Encoding applies every rule to every word, in order. The work grows with the number of rules times the amount of text. A real vocabulary has fifty thousand tokens. This is a teaching implementation and it behaves like one.

Training has a similar shape: it recounts all the pairs after every merge.

None of these are bugs. They are the differences between a tokenizer written to be read and one used to train a model. The next section removes them one at a time.

## 1.6 `mbpe`: a production-grade tokenizer

`mbpe` is the tokenizer the rest of the book uses. It is byte-level, compatible with `tiktoken`'s file format, and written in Mojo with Python bindings. Its design has one organizing idea:

> The BPE core knows only BPE. Everything that distinguishes one tokenizer family from another lives in a pre-tokenizer.

We take them in the same order.

### Fix one: bytes instead of characters

`mbpe` does not start from the characters in the training text. It starts from all 256 possible byte values. Any text, in any language, is a sequence of bytes. There is nothing to be unknown.

The mapping below is how bytes are *displayed*. Printable ASCII and most of Latin-1 display as themselves. The remaining bytes, control characters, the space, and a few others, are assigned code points starting at 256, in order.

*`mbpe/bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 216–231 (inside `BPETokenizer.__init__`)*

```mojo
        var n = 0
        for b in range(256):
            var printable = False
            if b >= 0x21 and b <= 0x7E:
                printable = True
            elif b >= 0xA1 and b <= 0xAC:
                printable = True
            elif b >= 0xAE and b <= 0xFF:
                printable = True
            if printable:
                self.byte_to_cp[b] = b
            else:

                var cp = 256 + n
                self.byte_to_cp[b] = cp
                n += 1
```

Count: the bytes `0x00` to `0x20` are all non-printable, and space is the 33rd of them. It lands on code point 288, which is U+0120, which is `Ġ`. The marker in `simple_bpe`'s pre-tokenizer was never arbitrary. It is how GPT-2 shows a space.

Here is the `mbpe` version of the test from section 1.5. Same corpus, same unseen input:

*`mbpe/tests/test_tokenizer.mojo`, lines 7–22*

```mojo
def test_byte_level_no_unk() raises:
    """Verify unknown byte sequences produce 3 distinct non-zero token IDs.
    Train on "abc", encode "xyz" — since our vocab is byte-level, every
    byte (including those unseen during training) maps to a known token ID.
    No UNK token is ever produced.
    """
    var corpus = List[String]()
    corpus.append(String("abc"))
    var tok = BPETokenizer()
    tok.train(corpus, 300)
    var ids = tok.encode(String("xyz"))
    assert_equal(len(ids), 3)
    assert_true(ids[0] != 0)
    assert_true(ids[1] != 0)
    assert_true(ids[2] != 0)
    assert_equal(tok.decode(ids), "xyz")
```

Three IDs, none of them zero, and the text decodes back exactly.

### Fix two: a swappable pre-tokenizer

The pre-tokenizer is a Mojo trait. The core is generic over it, and the choice is made at compile time.

*`mbpe/bpe/pretokenizer.mojo`, lines 357–380 (the start of the `PreTokenizer` trait)*

```mojo
trait PreTokenizer(Movable & Defaultable & Deinitable & Writable):

    comptime byte_map: ByteMapping

    @staticmethod
    @always_inline
    def byte_to_id(b: Int) -> Int:

        return b

    @staticmethod
    @always_inline
    def id_to_byte(rank: Int) -> Int:

        return rank

    def split[
        mut: Bool, //, origin: Origin[mut=mut]
    ](self, text: StringSpan[origin]) raises -> List[StringSpan[origin]]:

        var result = List[StringSpan[origin]]()
        result.reserve(1)
        result.append(text)
        return result^
```

The default `split` returns the whole text as one piece. Each implementation also supplies a name and its special tokens:

*`mbpe/bpe/pretokenizer.mojo`, lines 390–396 (the end of the trait)*

```mojo
    @staticmethod
    def name() -> String:
        ...

    @staticmethod
    def special_tokens() -> Dict[String, Int]:
        return Dict[String, Int]()
```

The GPT-2 implementation tries a fixed list of matchers at each position, in this order: a contraction like `'s`, a run of letters, a run of digits, a run of punctuation, then the three whitespace cases. The first that matches wins, and the text is cut there.

*`mbpe/bpe/pretokenizer.mojo`, lines 588–631 (`GPT2Pretokenizer`)*

```mojo
    @staticmethod
    @always_inline
    def _best_match[
        origin: Origin, //
    ](span: Span[UInt8, origin], pos: Int) raises -> Int:

        var m = GPT2Pretokenizer._match_contraction(span, pos)
        if m > 0:
            return m
        m = GPT2Pretokenizer._match_letter_run(span, pos)
        if m > 0:
            return m
        m = GPT2Pretokenizer._match_digit_run(span, pos)
        if m > 0:
            return m
        m = GPT2Pretokenizer._match_punct_run(span, pos)
        if m > 0:
            return m
        m = Self.match_trailing_all_ws(span, pos)
        if m > 0:
            return m
        m = Self.match_ws_not_before_nonws(span, pos)
        if m > 0:
            return m
        return Self.match_single_ws(span, pos)

    def split[
        mut: Bool, //, origin: Origin[mut=mut]
    ](self, text: StringSpan[origin]) raises -> List[StringSpan[origin]]:

        var result = List[StringSpan[origin]]()
        var n = text.byte_length()
        if n == 0:
            return result^
        var span = text.as_bytes()
        var pos = 0
        while pos < n:
            var best_len = GPT2Pretokenizer._best_match(span, pos)
            if best_len == 0:
                best_len = utf8_codepoint_byte_length(span[pos])
            var byte_span = span[pos : pos + best_len]
            result.append(StringSpan(unsafe_from_utf8=byte_span))
            pos += best_len
        return result^
```

These are hand-written byte scans, not a regular-expression engine. Together they implement GPT-2's splitting rules. GPT-4 and GPT-4o get their own implementations behind the same trait, and a new tokenizer family means a new implementation, not a change to the core.

This is also what "the GPT-2 tokenizer" means. Three things travel together: the pre-tokenizer, the vocabulary and the algorithm. Change any one and the IDs change. Load the right vocabulary under the wrong pre-tokenizer and the round trip still passes, because the bytes come back exactly. The IDs are wrong, and nothing complains. A green round trip proves byte handling, not agreement with the model's tokenizer.

### Fix three: merging by rank

`simple_bpe` replays all rules. `mbpe` asks a different question about each word: *of the adjacent pairs here, which one is the oldest rule?* Merge that one, then ask again. Stop when no adjacent pair has a rule.

This works because of how IDs are assigned. A merged token gets the next free ID, so a merged token's ID is also its rank: smaller means learned earlier. The lookup table stores `(left ID, right ID) -> merged ID`, and the merged ID doubles as the priority.

*`mbpe/bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 80–91 (`MergeLookup.set` and `.get`)*

```mojo
    @always_inline
    def set(mut self, id1: Int, id2: Int, merged_id: Int):
        if id1 < CACHE_SIZE and id2 < CACHE_SIZE:
            self._fast.unsafe_ptr()[unsafe_offset=(id1 << CACHE_SHIFT) | id2] = merged_id
        else:
            self._slow[(id1 << ENCODE_SHIFT) | id2] = merged_id

    @always_inline
    def get(self, id1: Int, id2: Int) -> Int:
        if id1 < CACHE_SIZE and id2 < CACHE_SIZE:
            return self._fast.unsafe_ptr()[unsafe_offset=(id1 << CACHE_SHIFT) | id2]
        return self._slow.get((id1 << ENCODE_SHIFT) | id2, -1)
```

IDs below a fixed limit go into a flat array indexed by the pair. The rest go into a dictionary. Most merges involve common tokens, so most lookups hit the flat array.

With that table, merging one word is a loop:

*`mbpe/bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 433–461 (`_merge_scan`)*

```mojo
    @always_inline
    def _merge_scan[origin: Origin](
        self,
        ptr: Pointer[UInt8, origin],
        n: Int,
        dst: Pointer[Int, MutAnyOrigin],
    ) -> Int:

        self._copy_word_ids(ptr, n, dst)
        var len = n

        while len >= 2:
            var best_rank = -1
            var best_a = -1
            var best_b = -1
            var best_m = -1
            for i in range(len - 1):
                var merged = self.lookup_table.get(dst[unsafe_offset=i], dst[unsafe_offset=i + 1])
                if merged >= 0 and (best_rank < 0 or merged < best_rank):

                    best_rank = merged
                    best_a = dst[unsafe_offset=i]
                    best_b = dst[unsafe_offset=i + 1]
                    best_m = merged
            if best_rank < 0:

                break
            len = merge_inplace(dst, len, best_a, best_b, best_m)
        return len
```

Find the lowest-ranked pair. Merge it everywhere in the word. Repeat. `merge_inplace` does the rewrite without allocating:

*`mbpe/bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 1029–1050 (`merge_inplace`)*

```mojo
@always_inline
def merge_inplace(
    buf: Pointer[Int, MutAnyOrigin],
    n: Int,
    a: Int,
    b: Int,
    m: Int,
) -> Int:

    var w = 0
    var i = 0
    while i < n:
        if i < n - 1 and buf[unsafe_offset=i] == a and buf[unsafe_offset=i + 1] == b:

            buf[unsafe_offset=w] = m
            i += 2
        else:

            buf[unsafe_offset=w] = buf[unsafe_offset=i]
            i += 1
        w += 1
    return w
```

Short words use this scan. Longer words use a variant built on a linked list and a heap, which avoids rescanning the whole word after each merge. The threshold is a constant, `SCAN_LIMIT`, in the source. The whole pipeline is a few lines:

*`mbpe/bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 539–567 (inside `encode_ordinary`)*

```mojo
        ref words = self.pt.split(text)

        var total_bytes = 0
        for word in words:
            total_bytes += word.byte_length()

        var result = IntArray(unsafe_uninit_length=total_bytes)
        var write_pos = 0
        var scratch = MergeScratch()
        var heap = BinaryHeap[Int]()
        for word in words:
            var ptr = word.unsafe_ptr()
            var n = word.byte_length()

            var dst = (result.unsafe_ptr().unsafe_offset(write_pos)).as_unsafe_any_origin()

            if n < 2:

                self._copy_word_ids(ptr, n, dst)
                write_pos += n
            elif n < SCAN_LIMIT:
                write_pos += self._merge_scan(ptr, n, dst)
            else:
                write_pos += self._merge_heap(ptr, n, dst, scratch, heap)

        scratch.free()

        result.resize(write_pos, 0)
        return result^
```

Split into words. For each word, copy its bytes into IDs and merge. Concatenate. That is the full encoder.

### The same algorithm, pinned by a test

The textbook BPE example is the 11-byte string `aaabdaaabac`. Three merges reduce it to five tokens. The `mbpe` test suite trains on it and checks every ID:

*`mbpe/tests/test_tokenizer.mojo`, lines 107–128 (`test_wikipedia_example`)*

```mojo
def test_wikipedia_example() raises:
    """Train with 3 merges on "aaabdaaabac" — the canonical BPE example
    from Wikipedia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte_pair_encoding).

    After 3 merges the 11-byte input should reduce to exactly 5 tokens:
      a=97, b=98, c=99, d=100 (ASCII)
      Z=aa=256, Y=ab|Za=257, X=ZY|Yb=258
    Final tokens: [258, 100, 258, 97, 99]
    """
    var corpus = List[String]()
    corpus.append(String("aaabdaaabac"))
    var tok = BPETokenizer()
    tok.train(corpus, 256 + 3)

    var ids = tok.encode(String("aaabdaaabac"))
    assert_equal(len(ids), 5)
    assert_equal(ids[0], 258)
    assert_equal(ids[1], 100)
    assert_equal(ids[2], 258)
    assert_equal(ids[3], 97)
    assert_equal(ids[4], 99)
    assert_equal(tok.decode(ids), "aaabdaaabac")
```

The bytes keep their ASCII values: `a` is 97, `b` is 98, `c` is 99, `d` is 100. Merged tokens start at 256, because 0 to 255 are taken by the bytes. The first merge, `aa`, becomes 256. The third merge builds on the first two and becomes 258. Same loop as the napkin, now over bytes, and the IDs land where the textbook says they should.

### Why replaying by rank is correct

A merged token's bytes are always its two parents' bytes joined. Its ID is also larger than either parent's ID. So the lowest-ranked pair in a word is always the one training would have applied first, and every merge finds its parents already built.

Take three rules, learned in this order: `l o` becomes `lo`, `lo w` becomes `low`, `low e` becomes `lowe`. Encode `lowering`, a word those rules were never trained on:

```text
l o w e r i n g
lo w e r i n g       rule 1
low e r i n g        rule 2
lowe r i n g         rule 3
```

The learned prefix fires on unseen input because the prefix is shared. That is why a bounded vocabulary covers unbounded text. The repo checks the invariant directly: `test_tiktoken_merge_consistency`, in `mbpe/main.mojo`.

### What byte-level buys: the round trip

For any valid UTF-8 input, `decode(encode(s))` returns `s` exactly. That is the point of starting from 256 bytes.

Two things can look like exceptions and are worth knowing. A vocabulary trained on one kind of text, meeting another, produces more and smaller pieces. Quality drops. Correctness does not. The real asterisks sit outside BPE: special tokens such as `<|endoftext|>`, which `encode` recognizes and `encode_ordinary` treats as plain text, and invalid UTF-8, where the bytes survive but a Mojo `String` may refuse to hold them.

### Training, briefly

`mbpe` also trains. Its training keeps the pair counts up to date as merges happen instead of recounting from scratch, which is what makes it practical on large corpora. The README shows the call:

```mojo
from bpe.tokenizer import BPETokenizer
from bpe.pretokenizer import GPT2Pretokenizer


def main() raises:
    var tok = BPETokenizer[GPT2Pretokenizer]()
    var corpus = List[String]()
    corpus.append("hello world")
    tok.train(corpus, 300)
```

*Source: the training example in the `mbpe` README.*

One thing training does *not* attempt: matching the exact merges OpenAI's training run produced from its private data. That is not something we can reproduce. What we can do is load the vocabularies OpenAI published.

## 1.7 Reading and writing `tiktoken` files

A `.tiktoken` file is one line per token: the token's bytes in base64, a space, and its rank. The first lines of `mbpe/data/gpt2.tiktoken`:

```text
IQ== 0
Ig== 1
Iw== 2
```

`IQ==` is the single byte `!`. GPT-2 gives it rank 0, then `"` rank 1, then `#` rank 2. The file lists the 256 byte tokens first, in GPT-2's own order, followed by the merges in the order they were learned.

Loading it is one call:

*`mbpe/bpe/tokenizer.mojo`, lines 1059–1070 (`Tokenizers`)*

```mojo
struct Tokenizers:

    comptime gpt2 = GPT2Pretokenizer
    comptime cl100k = GPT4Pretokenizer[ByteMapping.SEQUENTIAL]
    comptime o200k = GPT4Pretokenizer[ByteMapping.SHUFFLED]

    @staticmethod
    def get[T: PreTokenizer](filename: String = "") raises -> BPETokenizer[T]:
        var fname = T.name() if filename.byte_length() == 0 else filename
        var tok = BPETokenizer[T]()
        tok.load_tiktoken(_find_data_dir() + "/" + fname + ".tiktoken")
        return tok^
```

`Tokenizers.get[Tokenizers.gpt2]()` reads `gpt2.tiktoken` from the directory named by the `MBPE_DATA_DIR` environment variable, or from `./data/` if it is unset. Because the file format is shared, the same loader handles the other vocabularies in `mbpe/data`.

Two facts about the file are easy to miss. It has 50,256 lines for a vocabulary of 50,257, because `<|endoftext|>` is not in it: the file holds only mergeable tokens, and the pre-tokenizer declares the specials. And it has no merge list. The merges are recovered from the ranks, using the invariant from the last section. The ranks are the history.

## 1.8 Checking our work against GPT-2

A tokenizer that disagrees with the published one is not GPT-2's tokenizer. So we check.

Here is a short sample sentence, one that mixes punctuation, a special token, and a word no vocabulary contains. It is the test sentence used in Sebastian Raschka's *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)*, chapter 2, which makes the result easy to compare if you have the book:

```python
import mbpe

tokenizer = mbpe.get_encoding("gpt2")

text = (
    "Hello, do you like tea? <|endoftext|> "
    "In the sunlit terraces of someunknownPlace."
)
ids = tokenizer.encode(text, allowed_special={"<|endoftext|>"})
print(ids)
```

The expected output:

```text
[15496, 11, 466, 345, 588, 8887, 30, 220, 50256, 554, 262, 4252, 18250, 8812, 2114, 286, 617, 34680, 27271, 13]
```

Three things to see in it.

`50256` is `<|endoftext|>`, the last ID in a vocabulary of 50,257. It is a special token: it never comes out of merging, and the pre-tokenizer lists it separately.

`220` is a lone space, the one before `<|endoftext|>`. Spaces normally ride on the front of the next word (`" In"` is `554`), but a space with nothing to attach to is a token of its own.

`someunknownPlace`, a word no vocabulary has, still encodes. It is three pieces: ` some`, `unknown`, `Place`. No `<UNK>`, no error.

## 1.9 From IDs to training examples

The model is trained to predict the next token. So the training data is built from the same list twice, once as it is and once shifted left by one.

Take any run of IDs from the encoded text. Call it `ids`. The input is `ids` without its last element. The target is `ids` without its first. Position by position:

| position | input (what the model sees) | target (what it must predict) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | `ids[0]` | `ids[1]` |
| 1 | `ids[0], ids[1]` | `ids[2]` |
| 2 | `ids[0], ids[1], ids[2]` | `ids[3]` |

Every position is a small prediction problem, and one list of N+1 IDs provides N of them at once. The next chapter turns these lists into tensors and gives each ID a vector, and the library that does it is `tenmo`.

## 1.10 Pitfalls

**Stale `_mbpe.so`.** Python imports the compiled library, not the Mojo source. Edit Mojo, rerun Python, and nothing changes until you rebuild.

**Forgetting `-I .`.** Code that does `from bpe.tokenizer import ...` needs the include path. The test files under `mbpe/tests/` need it, as the `mbpe` README shows. `main.mojo` at the repository root does not.

**Data directory.** The Mojo loader reads the `MBPE_DATA_DIR` environment variable, then falls back to `./data/`. Run from the repository root or set the variable.

**`encode` versus `encode_ordinary`.** The first recognizes special tokens in the text. The second treats them as ordinary characters. If you expected one ID for `<|endoftext|>` and got seven, you called the second.

**Specials across save and load.** `save_tiktoken` skips special tokens by design. A tokenizer that loads back without them is behaving correctly.

**Wrong pre-tokenizer for the file.** Loads fine, round-trips fine, wrong IDs. See the end of "A swappable pre-tokenizer".

## Summary

- A language model reads integers. A tokenizer turns text into integers and back.
- Word-level vocabularies fail on unseen words. Character-level sequences are too long. Subword tokenization sits between them.
- BPE builds its vocabulary by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair, and records each merge as a rule. Encoding applies the rules in the order they were learned.
 - `simple_bpe` implements this in a few hundred readable lines, and fails in three ways: unseen characters become `<UNK>`, its pre-tokenizer handles only spaces and periods, and encoding replays every rule.
- `mbpe` starts from all 256 bytes, splits words with a swappable pre-tokenizer, merges by rank, and reads and writes the `tiktoken` file format.
- Loaded with the published GPT-2 vocabulary, `mbpe` produces GPT-2's token IDs.
- The GPT-2 tokenizer is three things together: a pre-tokenizer, a vocabulary and the algorithm. A round trip proves the bytes, not the agreement.
- The model sees IDs, nothing else. Its next-token training data is the same ID list, shifted by one.

Eleven characters in, two integers out. Now you know where the two integers come from.
